# [Before we begin]{.num}A letter to you

Maybe you are reading this a few days after the email.

The one that said your role had been "impacted by restructuring." Or the one that said, kindly and briefly, that they had "decided to go in a different direction." Maybe there was no email at all. Maybe it was a slow ending: a business you poured three years of savings into that never quite caught, a marriage that quietly ran out of road, a move to a new city that took you away from everyone who knew your name. Maybe you stepped away to raise children or care for a parent, and now that you want to step back in, the door looks smaller than you remember.

However you got here, I want to say something first, before any advice or exercises.

**You have not failed at life. You are in the middle of it.**

Most of the women you admire on LinkedIn have had at least one season like the one you are in now. You don't see it, because nobody posts the month they cried in the car outside the bank, or the afternoons they spent refreshing their inbox. You see the announcement at the end: *"I'm thrilled to share that I'm starting a new position..."* What you don't see is the long, unglamorous middle that made it possible.

This book is about that middle.

It will not tell you that everything happens for a reason, or that you just need to think positively and the universe will provide. Positive thinking, the way we mean it at Think Positive Always, is not pretending things are fine. It is the discipline of looking at things exactly as they are and still choosing the next useful step. It is hope with its sleeves rolled up.

Here is how to use what follows:

- **Read it slowly.** One chapter a day is plenty. Some chapters will land immediately; others will make more sense in a month.
- **Do the exercises on paper.** Each chapter ends with a short "Try this" section. Writing by hand slows your thinking down in a way that typing does not. This book is designed to be printed, so you can write directly in it.
- **Be honest, not harsh.** You will be asked to look at hard things. Look at them the way you would look at a friend's situation: clearly, but kindly.
- **Use the plan at the end.** Chapter 12 turns everything into thirty small days of action. If you only have energy for one part of this book, start there.

You are allowed to start again. As many times as you need to.

Let's begin.

# [Chapter 1]{.num}Starting again is not starting over

When Wanjiru was let go from her role as a regional marketing manager, the first thing she said to her sister was, "Eleven years, and I'm back at zero."

It is a very common thing to say. It is also not true.

Zero is where you start when you have nothing: no skills, no experience, no relationships, no self-knowledge. Wanjiru had eleven years of knowing how to launch a product in four markets at once. She knew how to calm an angry distributor and how to get a finance team to approve a budget in the last week of the quarter. She had a contact list of people who trusted her work. She knew she did her best thinking in the morning and that she hated open-plan offices. She knew, after one bad manager, exactly what kind of leader she would never become.

She was not starting over. She was starting *again*, with everything she had learned still in her hands.

## The difference matters

"Starting over" carries the feeling of erasure, as if the previous chapter is wiped out and you must earn your place from scratch. That feeling is heavy, and it makes people freeze. Why bother, if everything you built has disappeared?

"Starting again" carries continuity. Same woman, same experience, new attempt. It recognises that the ending was real and that it hurt, and also that you walk into the next chapter carrying more than you had when you walked into the last one.

This is not wordplay. The language you use about your situation shapes what you believe is possible, and what you believe is possible shapes what you are willing to try. If you believe you are at zero, you will apply for roles three levels below your experience "just to get in." You will price your services as if you were a beginner. You will introduce yourself apologetically. If you believe you are starting again, you will look for the next room that fits the person you have become.

## Three things that are true at the same time

In the early days of a restart, your mind may swing between two extremes. One moment: *This is the best thing that ever happened, I was never happy there anyway.* The next: *I've ruined everything and I'll never recover.* Both are your nervous system trying to make sense of a sudden change.

The steadier place to stand is in the middle, where several things are true at once:

1. **This is a real loss.** Income, identity, routine, colleagues, status, a future you had pictured. It is right to feel it.
2. **This is not the whole story.** Your worth was not on that payroll. Your abilities did not leave with your access card.
3. **This is also an opening.** Not because pain is secretly good, but because endings create room that busy lives rarely allow: room to ask what you actually want next.

You do not have to feel all three at once. You only have to stop insisting that one of them cancels the others.

## A word about speed

On LinkedIn, everything looks fast. Someone gets laid off on Monday and announces a new role on Friday. Someone quits to start a business and is "thrilled to announce" their first big client two weeks later.

Your restart does not have to keep that pace. Some restarts take weeks. Many take months. Some, especially after illness, divorce or caregiving, take a year or longer to settle. That is not a sign that you are doing it wrong. It is a sign that you are a person and not a press release.

Speed is not the goal. Direction is.

# [Chapter 2]{.num}Name what ended

Before you can build something new, it helps to be honest about what has ended. This sounds obvious, but many of us skip it. We go straight to updating the CV, booking coffee meetings, and telling everyone we are "excited about what's next," while a quieter part of us has not yet caught up.

Grief is not only for death. You can grieve a job, a business, a relationship, a city, a version of yourself, or a future you were sure you would have. Psychologists sometimes call this *disenfranchised grief*: grief that people around you do not recognise as grief, so you feel you have no right to it. "At least you got a package." "At least you didn't have kids together." "At least you can try again." Every "at least" is well meant, and every one of them tells you to hurry up.

You are allowed to take a moment.

## What you actually lost

Most endings involve several losses tangled together. When we untangle them, they become easier to carry, and easier to replace one by one.

Amara ran a small events company in Nairobi for six years. When she finally closed it, she thought she had lost "the business." When she sat down and wrote it out, she realised she had lost:

- Her income and her savings buffer.
- The title "founder," which she loved saying at dinners.
- A team of four people who felt like family.
- The thrill of event days.
- Her daily routine and reason to get dressed.
- The story she had been telling her parents, who had warned her against leaving her bank job.

Some of those losses needed money to fix. Some needed time. One of them, the story she told her parents, only needed a single honest conversation, which turned out to be much less frightening than she had imagined. And one of them, the thrill of event days, pointed straight toward what she would do next: she went on to run events for a large organisation, without the payroll stress, and found she enjoyed it more.

You cannot replace a loss you have not named.

## Common hidden losses

As you think about your own ending, look out for the losses that often hide behind the obvious one:

- **Identity.** "I'm the head of..." "I'm a founder." "I'm a wife." When the title goes, the question "Who am I now?" can feel very loud.
- **Structure.** Work gives us places to be, people to see and reasons to wake up. Without it, days can blur.
- **Belonging.** The WhatsApp groups, the office jokes, the people who understood your world without explanation.
- **Status.** We don't like to admit it, but being respected matters. Losing a role can feel like being demoted in the eyes of everyone you know.
- **Certainty.** Perhaps the biggest one. You knew what next year looked like. Now you don't.
- **The imagined future.** The promotion you were in line for. The house you would have bought. The person you thought you would grow old with.

## Letting yourself feel it, without moving in

There is a difference between feeling grief and living inside it. One useful practice is to give it a container: a time and a place.

Some women write for fifteen minutes each morning, just letting the feelings out on paper. Some go for a long walk on Sunday and let themselves be sad for the whole walk, then come home and make dinner. Some talk to a counsellor once a week. The container says: *this matters, and it does not get to run my entire day.*

If you notice that the sadness is not lifting after several weeks, that you cannot sleep or eat, that you feel hopeless most of the time, or that you are having thoughts of not wanting to be here, please talk to a doctor or a mental health professional. That is not weakness. It is exactly what a strong person does when something is too heavy to carry alone.

# [Chapter 3]{.num}The story you tell about the fall

Two women are made redundant on the same day by the same company.

The first tells herself: *They chose me because I wasn't good enough. Everyone will know. At my age, nobody will hire me.*

The second tells herself: *They cut a whole department to save costs. It wasn't about my work. This is painful and inconvenient, and I've survived painful and inconvenient things before.*

Same event. Two completely different stories. And within a month, those stories will have shaped very different behaviour. The first woman may avoid her network out of shame, apply to only a handful of roles, and walk into interviews expecting rejection. The second may reach out widely, ask for referrals, and walk into interviews as a capable professional between roles, which is exactly what she is.

Our stories are not just descriptions of what happened. They are instructions for what we do next.

## How to catch your story

Your story usually shows up in three places:

- **The first thing you say when someone asks what happened.** Listen to yourself. Do you apologise? Do you over-explain? Do you blame yourself for things outside your control?
- **The thoughts that arrive at 3 a.m.** These tend to be the harshest version: *You're a fraud. You'll never recover. You wasted everything.*
- **The things you avoid.** If you are avoiding a former colleague, a networking event, or even your own LinkedIn feed, ask what story the avoidance is protecting.

When you catch a painful thought, try asking these four questions:

1. **Is this a fact or an interpretation?** "My role was eliminated" is a fact. "Because I'm not good enough" is an interpretation.
2. **What else could be true?** List at least two other explanations.
3. **What would I say to a friend who told me this about herself?** You would probably be far gentler, and more accurate.
4. **Which story helps me take the next useful step?** Not the most cheerful story. The most useful one.

## The comparison trap

There is a particular kind of pain that comes from scrolling LinkedIn during a hard season. Everyone seems to be promoted, speaking at conferences, closing funding rounds, or "humbled" by some award. You close the app feeling smaller than when you opened it.

Remember what you are looking at: a highlight reel, carefully edited, often written in the most flattering light possible. You are comparing your behind-the-scenes to everyone else's trailer.

Three practical boundaries can help:

- **Use the platform with a purpose.** Open it to do one thing (send three messages, comment on two posts, check one job listing) and then close it.
- **Mute, don't torture yourself.** You can mute people whose updates consistently leave you feeling worse, without unfollowing or explaining.
- **Follow the honest ones.** Seek out people who talk openly about career gaps, pivots and failures. There are more of them than you think, and they will remind you that your season is normal.

## A kinder, truer story

You are not trying to invent a fairy tale. You are trying to write the version of events that is both *true* and *useful*. It often follows this shape:

> "Here is what happened. Here is what I learned. Here is what I'm moving toward."

For Wanjiru, it became: *"My department was restructured after eleven years. I'm proud of what I built there: I led launches across four markets. I'm now looking for a senior marketing role where I can build a team from the ground up."*

Notice what is missing: shame, over-explanation and apology. Notice what is present: the facts, the value she brings and a clear direction.

# [Chapter 4]{.num}Take inventory: what you still carry

After an ending, our attention naturally goes to what is missing. That is human. But to build again, you need an accurate picture of what you have. Think of it as standing in your kitchen after a long trip, checking the cupboards before you plan meals. You might be surprised by how much is still there.

## Your five cupboards

**1. Skills.** These are the things you can do. Separate them into *technical skills* (financial modelling, coding, writing grant proposals, managing a supply chain) and *human skills* (negotiating, calming tense rooms, explaining complicated ideas simply, building trust quickly). Women in particular tend to undervalue the second group because it came "naturally." It did not. You practised it for years.

**2. Experience and evidence.** What have you actually done? Which results can you point to? Projects you led, problems you solved, money you saved or made, people you developed. Numbers help, but stories help too.

**3. Relationships.** Former managers, colleagues, clients, classmates, mentors, people from your church or chama or alumni group. Most opportunities arrive through people, and you probably know more of them than you think.

**4. Resources.** Savings, severance, a partner's income, a family home you can stay in, a laptop, a quiet corner to work in, a free course you have access to. Some restarts have more resources than others, and that is not a moral judgement. It is just information.

**5. Self-knowledge.** This is the cupboard nobody talks about, and it may be the most valuable. You now know things about yourself that you did not know before: what drains you, what energises you, what you will not tolerate again, what you are capable of surviving.

## The evidence file

One of the most practical things you can do this week is to start an *evidence file*. It can be a notebook, a phone note or a folder on your laptop. In it, collect:

- Thank-you messages and kind emails from colleagues or clients.
- Performance reviews with good feedback.
- Results you delivered, written as simply as possible: *"Reduced customer complaints by a third in six months."*
- Moments you were proud of, even if nobody else noticed.

On the days when the harsh story is loud, open the file. It is very hard to argue with evidence.

## Finding the thread

When you look across your cupboards, look for the *thread*: the thing you were doing, in some form, in every role, project or chapter of your life. Perhaps you were always the one who organised people. Perhaps you were always the one who translated between the technical team and the customers. Perhaps you were always the one others came to when things fell apart.

Your thread is not a job title. It is a pattern of value. And it can travel into many different next chapters.

# [Chapter 5]{.num}Small, unglamorous beginnings

Most restarts do not begin with a dramatic moment. They begin with a woman at a kitchen table on a Tuesday, sending one email she has been avoiding for a week.

We tend to wait for motivation before we act. But motivation is usually the *result* of action, not the cause. You do something small, it goes slightly better than you feared, and you feel a little more able to do the next thing. That is momentum. It cannot be thought into existence. It has to be walked into.

## The two-percent rule

When everything feels heavy, ask: *What is a two-percent step?* Not the whole thing. Not even a big chunk. Just a small, specific action that moves you very slightly forward.

- Not "update my CV," but "rewrite the top summary of my CV."
- Not "network," but "message one former colleague to say hello."
- Not "start a business," but "write down three problems I could solve for people who would pay."
- Not "get my life together," but "make my bed and go for a fifteen-minute walk."

Two-percent steps feel almost silly. That is the point. They are small enough that your fear does not bother to fight them.

## Build a simple day

When an ending takes away your structure, building a new one is one of the fastest ways to feel steadier. It does not need to be elaborate. A good restart day often has just four anchors:

1. **A start time.** Get up at roughly the same time each day, even without a commute.
2. **One focused block.** Ninety minutes on the most important task: applications, outreach, product building or learning.
3. **Movement.** A walk, a stretch, a dance in your living room. Your body is carrying this stress too.
4. **A stop time.** Decide when the work day ends. Job searching or business building can quietly expand to fill every waking hour, which leads to exhaustion rather than results.

## Track the right things

In a restart, the results you want (the offer, the client, the signed contract) are often outside your direct control. If you only measure those, you will feel like you are failing for weeks, even while you are doing everything right.

Instead, track the *inputs* you control: messages sent, applications submitted, coffee chats held, pages written, hours spent learning. Inputs lead to outcomes, eventually. And on the slow weeks, a page full of completed inputs reminds you that you are moving.

## Done is a beginning

You may be someone who likes to get things right before showing them to anyone. That instinct has probably served you well in your career. In a restart, it can become a trap. The perfect CV, the perfect LinkedIn post, the perfect business name: you can polish for months while the world moves on without you.

Let "good enough to share" be your standard for now. You can improve it after it is out in the world. The first version of almost everything is a little rough. That is how everyone starts.

# [Chapter 6]{.num}Money, fear and the practical middle

Let's be honest about something many personal-growth books skip: money.

For most women, a restart is not only an emotional journey. It is a financial one. Rent is due. School fees are due. Family members may depend on you. Fear about money can make it very difficult to think clearly about anything else, and that fear is not a mindset problem. It is a reasonable response to a real situation.

You do not need to pretend it isn't there. You need to look at it directly so it gets smaller.

*A quick note: this chapter offers general guidance, not personal financial advice. If your situation is complex, consider speaking to a qualified financial adviser.*

## Know your number

Anxiety thrives on vagueness. "I'm running out of money" is terrifying. "I have enough to cover my essentials for four and a half months" is something you can plan around.

Sit down with your bank statements, M-Pesa history or budgeting app, and work out three numbers:

1. **Essential monthly costs.** Rent or mortgage, food, utilities, transport, school fees, loan repayments, medical cover and support you give to family.
2. **Available resources.** Savings, severance, any expected income and money you can realistically access.
3. **Your runway.** Divide resources by essential monthly costs. That is roughly how many months you have.

Then ask: what could make the runway longer? Can any costs pause? Can any bills be renegotiated? Can you sell something you no longer need? Is there short-term or freelance work that could bring in some income while you pursue the bigger goal?

## The bridge

Sometimes the wisest move is a *bridge*: a role, contract or side income that is not your dream but pays the bills while you build toward it. There is no shame in a bridge. Many women who now run successful businesses consulted, taught, or worked a part-time job for a year while the business grew.

A bridge is not a step backward if you know where it leads.

## Fear is a signal, not a steering wheel

Fear is useful. It tells you that something matters and that there is risk. Listen to it; it may be pointing at something you need to plan for. But don't let it drive. Fear tends to make you either freeze (doing nothing, hoping it resolves itself) or panic (accepting the first thing that comes along, even if it's a poor fit).

When you notice fear, try writing down:

- **What exactly am I afraid of?** Be specific.
- **What's the realistic worst case?** Not the catastrophic movie version; the realistic version.
- **What would I do if it happened?** Usually you have more options than fear wants you to see.
- **What can I do today to make it less likely?**

## Asking for what you're worth

When money feels tight, there is a strong pull to accept less: a lower salary, a lower day rate, a smaller title. Sometimes that is a sensible strategic choice. But be careful that it is a *choice* and not just fear speaking.

Before any negotiation, research the market rate for the role or service. Ask peers. Look at salary surveys for your industry and region. Then decide your *walk-away number*, the figure below which you will say no, before you are in the room. It is much easier to hold a line you drew while calm.

# [Chapter 7]{.num}How to talk about your restart

"So, what are you doing these days?"

For many women in a restart, this question feels like a small ambush. At a wedding, in a church corridor, in a WhatsApp group, in an interview. You stumble, over-explain, and walk away replaying the conversation.

You can prepare for this. Not with a rehearsed speech, but with a few honest, simple sentences that you are comfortable saying.

## The three-sentence answer

A good answer has three parts: **where you've been, what you're doing now, and what you're looking for.** For example:

> "I spent eight years in banking operations, most recently leading a team of twelve. My role was restructured earlier this year, so I'm taking the chance to move into risk and compliance. If you know anyone in that space, I'd love an introduction."

> "I ran my own catering business for five years. I closed it last year. It taught me a huge amount about running a P&L and managing suppliers. Now I'm looking at operations roles in hospitality."

> "I took three years out to care for my mum. I'm ready to come back, and I'm looking for HR roles where I can use my people experience, ideally in a hybrid set-up."

Notice that the last sentence invites help. People generally like to be useful, but they can only help if they know what you're looking for.

## Talking about a gap

Career gaps are far more common than they used to be, and many employers now understand that life does not follow a straight line. Still, you may be asked about one. A few principles:

- **Be brief and factual.** "I took two years to care for my children." You don't need to justify it.
- **Mention what you kept alive.** Courses, volunteering, freelance work, community leadership, reading in your field.
- **Point forward.** Spend most of your answer on why you're ready now and what you'll bring.

## Announcing it on LinkedIn

You don't *have* to post about your restart. But for many women, a short, honest post opens more doors than months of quiet applications. Your network can't help with something they don't know about.

A simple structure that works well:

1. **The news, plainly.** "After nine years at X, my role was made redundant last month."
2. **A moment of gratitude or reflection.** One or two lines, no more.
3. **What you bring.** Two or three specific strengths or results.
4. **What you're looking for.** Be as specific as you can: role type, industry, location or remote.
5. **The ask.** "If you know of anything, or anyone I should speak to, I'd be so grateful for an introduction."

You do not need to perform positivity, and you do not need to share every feeling. Honest and hopeful is enough. And you can turn on the "Open to Work" setting privately, visible only to recruiters, if you prefer.

## Scripts for reaching out

Many of us dread messaging people we haven't spoken to in a while. Here are three you can adapt:

**To a former colleague:**
"Hi Grace, it's been a while! I've been thinking about our time on the Q3 launch. I'm currently exploring my next role in product marketing and would love to hear how things are going for you. Would you be open to a quick 20-minute call in the next couple of weeks?"

**To someone in a field you want to enter:**
"Hi Leah, I've been following your posts on supply chain sustainability and found your piece on local sourcing really useful. I'm moving into this area after ten years in procurement. Would you be willing to share 15 minutes of advice on how you made your own transition?"

**Asking for an introduction:**
"Hi James, I saw you're connected to Achieng at [company]. I'm very interested in their operations team. Would you feel comfortable introducing us? I've attached a two-line summary you could forward, to make it easy."

# [Chapter 8]{.num}Your circle

Nobody restarts entirely alone. Even the women who look like they did it all themselves had someone: a friend who reviewed the CV, a former boss who made a call, a mother who looked after the children during interviews, a stranger on LinkedIn who replied to a message.

One of the most important things you can do in a restart is to build your circle on purpose.

## The five people you need

Think about your circle as having five roles. One person can play more than one, and some roles might be filled by a group.

- **The listener.** Someone you can be completely honest with, who doesn't try to fix it, just lets you talk.
- **The cheerleader.** Someone who believes in you loudly, especially on the days you can't.
- **The truth-teller.** Someone who will tell you kindly when your CV is weak, when you're avoiding something, or when you're aiming too low.
- **The connector.** Someone who knows lots of people and enjoys introducing them.
- **The guide.** Someone who has done what you're trying to do, a few steps ahead of you.

Look at your list. Which roles are filled? Which are empty? An empty role is not a problem. It's a direction for your next conversation.

## Asking for help without feeling like a burden

Many capable women find it far easier to give help than to ask for it. We worry about being a bother, about looking needy, about owing people.

Three things to remember:

1. **Most people like to help.** It feels good to be useful, and to be asked for advice is a compliment.
2. **Specific asks are easier to say yes to.** "Can you help me find a job?" is heavy. "Would you look over my CV summary and tell me if it's clear?" is easy.
3. **You will return the favour.** Maybe not to the same person, maybe not right away. But you will be in a position to help someone else one day. That's how circles work.

## Protect yourself from the energy drainers

There may also be people who, without meaning to, make your restart harder. The relative who keeps asking "Have you found anything yet?" The friend who brings every conversation back to how risky your choices are. The former colleague who wants all the gossip about why you left.

You don't have to cut anyone off. But you can choose how much you share and when. A simple, warm line works well: *"I'm working on it, and I'll let you know when there's news. Tell me about you!"*

## Find your people

Communities of women going through similar transitions can be powerful. Look for professional associations, alumni groups, women's networks, online communities, chamas, mentorship programmes and peer circles. Being around others in the messy middle is a strong antidote to the feeling that you are the only one.

# [Chapter 9]{.num}When it gets hard again

Here is something no one tells you: restarts are rarely a straight line upward. You'll have a great week, with interviews lined up, a promising client conversation and real momentum. Then the interviews end in rejection, the client goes quiet, and you find yourself back on the sofa at 2 p.m. wondering why you bothered.

This is normal. It does not mean you're back at the beginning. It means you're in the middle of something real.

## Rejection is information, not a verdict

Rejection hurts. There's no way around that. But it helps to remember that most rejections are not about you as a person. They are about fit, timing, budgets, internal candidates, or a dozen factors you will never see.

After a rejection, give yourself a short window to feel it. A few hours, an evening. Then ask:

- Is there anything I can learn from this? (If you can, politely ask for feedback. Some will give it.)
- Is there anything I'd do differently?
- What's the next step?

And then take it. The best antidote to rejection is the next application, the next message, the next pitch.

## Rest is part of the work

When things are hard, many of us respond by pushing harder: more hours, more applications, more hustle. Sometimes that's needed. But often, what's needed is rest.

A depleted mind makes poor decisions. It writes flat cover letters, sounds tired in interviews and catastrophises about every silence. Rest is not quitting. It's maintenance.

Rest can look like a full day off with no job searching or business building at all. It can look like an evening with friends where you don't talk about work, or a long sleep, or an hour of something you love purely for its own sake.

## Signs you need more support

Sometimes "hard" becomes something heavier. Please reach out to a professional, such as your doctor, a counsellor or a therapist, if you notice:

- Sadness, emptiness or numbness that lasts most days for more than two weeks.
- Changes in sleep or appetite that you can't explain.
- Losing interest in things you usually enjoy.
- Using alcohol or other substances more than usual to cope.
- Thoughts of hurting yourself, or that people would be better off without you.

Getting help is one of the bravest and most practical things you can do. If you are in immediate danger, contact emergency services right away.

# [Chapter 10]{.num}The woman on the other side

Close your eyes for a moment, if you can, and picture yourself one year from now.

Not the fantasy version, with the corner office or the six-figure contract. The realistic, good version. The version where you have kept taking two-percent steps, kept reaching out, rested when you needed to, and kept going.

Where is she working? What does her morning look like? Who is around her? How does she feel when someone asks, "So, what do you do?"

And now, the most important question: *what did she do, back in this season, that made it possible?*

## She chose before she was certain

The woman on the other side did not wait until she was sure. She made decisions with incomplete information, the way everyone does. Some of those decisions were wrong, and she adjusted. But she kept choosing.

## She kept her standards and lowered her pride

She did not settle for a role or relationship that went against what she knew about herself. But she was willing to do unglamorous work, ask for help, take a bridge, and be a beginner at something new. Pride and standards are different things. She kept one and let go of the other.

## She told the truth about her story

She stopped apologising for her gap, her redundancy, her closed business, her divorce. She told a short, honest, forward-facing story, and she noticed that most people received it with respect.

## She was kind to herself on the bad days

She did not beat herself up into success. On the days she couldn't do much, she did a little, and forgave herself for the rest.

## She remembered she had done hard things before

This is not your first hard season. Somewhere in your history is a moment you thought you wouldn't get through, and you did. You may not have done it gracefully. You may have done it crying. But you did it, and you're here.

You will get through this one too.

# [A closing word]{.num}Begin again, as often as you need

There is a quiet kind of courage that doesn't make headlines. It's the courage to get up on a morning when nothing is guaranteed and do the next small thing anyway. To send the message. To show up to the interview. To open the laptop and keep building.

That courage is yours. It has been all along.

You don't have to have it all figured out. You only have to begin. And then, when you need to, begin again.
