# [Welcome]{.num}Why a reset, and why thirty days

You don't need a new life. You need a little more room to breathe in the one you have.

Perhaps you've noticed that your thoughts have become heavier lately. You wake up already running through everything that could go wrong. You read a colleague's promotion post and feel a sting before you feel happy for her. You say "I'm fine" so often it has become a reflex. You are capable, you are getting things done, and yet something inside feels tired, tight, or stuck.

That is what this workbook is for.

A mindset reset is not about forcing yourself to be cheerful. It's about noticing the patterns your mind runs on automatically, keeping the ones that serve you, and gently retraining the ones that don't. It is practical work, a bit like physiotherapy for your thinking. Small, repeated exercises, done consistently, slowly change how you respond to pressure, setbacks and everyday life.

## How the thirty days work

The workbook is organised into four weeks, each with a theme:

- **Week 1: Notice.** You'll learn to see your thoughts instead of being swept along by them.
- **Week 2: Reframe.** You'll practise questioning unhelpful thoughts and choosing more useful ones.
- **Week 3: Act.** You'll build small daily habits that make a positive mindset easier to sustain.
- **Week 4: Become.** You'll connect your new thinking to who you want to be, and plan how to keep going.

Each day has three parts:

1. **A short idea** to read (two minutes).
2. **A reflection** to write (five to ten minutes).
3. **One small action** to do during the day.

At the bottom of each page you'll rate your day. Over thirty days, those ratings tell a story.

## A few gentle rules

- **Don't aim for perfect.** If you miss a day, simply continue the next day. Don't restart from Day 1.
- **Write by hand if you can.** Print this workbook and keep it somewhere you'll see it, beside your bed or on your desk.
- **Be honest.** Nobody will read this but you.
- **Be kind.** You are not here to criticise yourself into a better mindset. That never works.

*A note on wellbeing: this workbook supports everyday wellbeing and personal growth. It is not a treatment for depression, anxiety or other mental health conditions. If you're struggling to cope, please speak to a doctor or qualified mental health professional.*

# [Day 1 · Notice]{.num}You are not your thoughts

Your mind produces thousands of thoughts every day. Most of them pass by unnoticed: what to cook, an email to send, a song stuck in your head. But some of them carry weight, and we treat them as facts. *I'm not good enough for this role. They probably think I'm annoying. I always mess things up.*

Here's the first shift: a thought is something your mind *does*, not something you *are*. You can notice it, the same way you notice a car passing outside your window. You don't have to get in.

### Reflect

Write down three thoughts you've had today that felt heavy. For each one, rewrite it starting with: *"I notice I'm having the thought that..."* How does it feel different?

<div class="write l8"></div>

# [Day 2 · Notice]{.num}Meet your inner critic

Most of us have a voice inside that comments on everything we do. It points out mistakes, predicts disaster and compares us unfavourably to others. Often, it sounds like someone from our past: a strict teacher, a parent, a harsh former boss.

# [Day 3 · Notice]{.num}Your thinking traps

When we're stressed, our minds fall into predictable patterns that distort reality. Psychologists call them *cognitive distortions*. Here are some of the most common:

**Catastrophising**: jumping to the worst possible outcome. **Mind-reading**: assuming you know what others think of you. **All-or-nothing**: if it's not perfect, it's a failure. **Should-ing**: "I should be further along by now." **Discounting the positive**: "That success doesn't count, I just got lucky." **Comparing**: measuring your inside against everyone else's outside.

### Reflect

Which two or three of these traps do you fall into most often? Write a recent example of each.

<div class="write l10"></div>

# [Day 4 · Notice]{.num}What your body is telling you

Your mindset doesn't live only in your head. Stress shows up in the body first: tight shoulders, a clenched jaw, shallow breathing, a knot in the stomach before a meeting. Often your body knows you're anxious before your mind admits it.

Learning to notice these signals gives you an early warning system. And because the body and mind are connected, calming the body is one of the fastest ways to calm the mind.

### Reflect

Where in your body do you feel stress? What situations trigger it most? What does your body feel like when you're truly relaxed?

<div class="write l8"></div>

### Today's action

# [Day 8 · Reframe]{.num}Fact or opinion?

Many of our most painful thoughts mix a fact with an interpretation. *"I didn't get the job"* is a fact. *"Because I'm not good enough"* is an interpretation. *"My manager didn't reply to my email"* is a fact. *"She's annoyed with me"* is an interpretation.

Separating facts from opinions is one of the simplest and most powerful mindset skills. It doesn't make every problem disappear, but it shrinks many of them back to their real size.

### Reflect

Choose one situation that's been bothering you. Draw a line down the middle of the space below. On the left, write only the facts. On the right, write your interpretations.

# [Day 9 · Reframe]{.num}The friend test

Imagine your closest friend came to you and said: *"I made a mistake in the presentation and now everyone thinks I'm incompetent."* You wouldn't say, "Yes, you're hopeless." You'd say something kind and probably more accurate: "One slip doesn't erase your track record."

Most of us speak to ourselves in ways we would never speak to anyone we love. The friend test simply asks: *what would I say to her?* And then: *can I say it to myself?*

### Reflect

Write down something harsh you've said to yourself recently. Then write what you'd say to a dear friend in the same situation.

# [Day 10 · Reframe]{.num}From "have to" to "get to"

Language shapes experience. *"I have to go to this meeting"* feels like a burden. *"I get to share my ideas with the leadership team"* feels like an opportunity. The meeting is the same.

This isn't about pretending everything is a gift. Some things really are chores. But many of our "have to"s are privileges in disguise: a job, a family, a body that works, a chance to learn.

# [Day 11 · Reframe]{.num}Yet

*"I'm not good at public speaking."* *"I don't understand finance."* *"I'm not a leader."*

Add one small word to the end of each sentence: **yet**. *I'm not good at public speaking yet.* Suddenly the sentence describes a stage of growth, not a fixed truth. Researchers call this a *growth mindset*: the belief that abilities develop with effort, good strategies and help from others.

# [Day 12 · Reframe]{.num}The other side of the comparison

Comparison is natural, and LinkedIn feeds it constantly. Someone your age is already a director. Someone who started after you just launched a business. It's easy to come away feeling behind.

Comparison can be useful when it shows you what's possible. It becomes harmful when it turns into a measure of your worth. A useful shift: turn envy into curiosity. Instead of "Why not me?" ask "What could I learn from how she did it?"

### Reflect

# [Day 13 · Reframe]{.num}Setbacks as data

Scientists don't call a failed experiment a failure. They call it data. It tells them what doesn't work, which brings them closer to what does.

What if you treated your setbacks the same way? The rejected proposal, the difficult conversation that went badly, the goal you didn't reach. Each one holds information about timing, approach, fit or skill. When you look for the data, the shame has less room to grow.

# [Day 15 · Act]{.num}Start the day on purpose

How you spend the first thirty minutes of your day often shapes the rest of it. If the first thing you do is reach for your phone and scroll through emails, news and other people's achievements, you begin the day reacting rather than choosing.

A simple morning anchor doesn't need to be long or elaborate. A glass of water, a few deep breaths, writing down one intention, a short prayer or a stretch. The point is to start on your own terms.

# [Day 19 · Act]{.num}Boundaries are a mindset tool

It's very hard to keep a positive mindset when your time and energy are constantly overstretched. Many women, especially high achievers, struggle to say no. We worry about disappointing people or seeming difficult.

But every yes to something that drains you is a no to something that matters. A boundary isn't a wall. It's a gate you control. A simple script: *"Thank you for thinking of me. I can't take this on right now, but I hope it goes well."*

### Reflect

# [Day 26 · Become]{.num}Your personal reset kit

There will be hard days after this workbook ends. That's not failure; it's life. The difference is that you'll have tools. A personal reset kit is a list of things you know work for you when your mindset slips.

Write it now, while you're feeling clear, so that on a hard day you don't have to think. You just follow your own instructions.

### Reflect

Create your reset kit: three thoughts that help, three actions that help, three people you can call, and one sentence you need to hear when things are hard.

# [Day 27 · Become]{.num}Celebrate yourself

Women are often very good at celebrating others and very poor at celebrating themselves. We move on to the next task before the last one has even settled. We minimise our wins: *it was a team effort*, *I just got lucky*, *it wasn't a big deal*.

Celebration isn't arrogance. It's accurate accounting. It teaches your brain that effort leads to reward, which makes the next effort easier.

### Reflect

List ten things you've achieved in the past year, big and small, professional and personal. Then list three things you're proud of from these 27 days.

<div class="write l12"></div>

### Today's action

Celebrate one win today in a way that feels good: a treat, a message to a friend or a moment of quiet pride.

# [Day 30 and beyond]{.num}Keep going

You've finished the workbook, but the reset is something you can return to whenever you need it. Many women repeat these thirty days every few months, or whenever life has knocked them off balance. You'll notice your answers change each time. That's growth.

## Ways to keep your mindset strong

- **Revisit your reset kit** whenever a hard day hits.
- **Keep your two favourite habits** from Week 3 going.
- **Repeat Week 1** whenever you feel stuck. Noticing is always the first step.
- **Find your people.** A mindset is easier to keep when you're around others working on theirs.
