# [Welcome]{.num}You are more than you've been told

Think of the most capable woman you know.

She might be a colleague who runs complicated projects without breaking a sweat, a friend who built a business from nothing, or your mother, who somehow held a household together on very little. Now imagine asking her, privately, whether she always feels confident.

Chances are she would laugh. And then she would tell you about the meetings where she stayed quiet even though she had the answer. The promotions she didn't apply for because she only met seven of the ten requirements. The nights she lay awake convinced that someone was about to discover she wasn't as good as everyone thought.

Self-doubt is not a sign that you're not capable. It is one of the most common experiences of capable women everywhere.

This workbook will not try to turn you into someone loud, brash or certain about everything. That's not confidence. That's performance. Instead, it will help you build two things that are quieter and much more durable:

- **Self-worth**: the settled belief that you have value as a person, regardless of your job title, your bank balance, your relationship status or how your last presentation went.
- **Confidence**: the trust that you can handle what's in front of you, learn what you don't know and recover when things go wrong.

Self-worth is the foundation. Confidence is what you build on it.

## How to use this workbook

There are eight modules. Each one includes a short explanation, examples drawn from the lives of real-world professional women, and exercises to complete on the page. You can work through one module a week for two months, or move faster if you prefer.

Print it. Write in it. Come back to it. The exercises work best when you're honest, specific and kind to yourself.

*The examples in this workbook are composites. This workbook is a personal-development resource and is not a substitute for therapy. If low self-worth is connected to trauma, abuse or ongoing depression, please consider working with a qualified counsellor alongside it.*

# [Module 1]{.num}Where your worth comes from

Ask most professional women what makes them valuable and they will list achievements: degrees, titles, results, the number of people who depend on them. Those things are real, and you should be proud of them. But if they are the *only* source of your worth, you're standing on shifting ground.

Jobs end. Businesses struggle. Bodies change. Relationships go through seasons. If your sense of worth rises and falls with each of those things, you will spend your life anxiously protecting your performance rather than enjoying it.

## Conditional and unconditional worth

Many of us learned early that we were valued *when*: when we got good grades, when we were helpful, when we didn't make a fuss, when we looked a certain way. This is *conditional* worth. It teaches us to earn our place constantly.

*Unconditional* worth is the understanding that you matter because you're a human being, not because of what you produce. It doesn't mean you stop striving. It means you strive from a place of security rather than fear.

Here's the difference in practice. Two women both receive critical feedback on a report.

- **Conditional worth:** *"I'm terrible at this. They must regret hiring me. I need to work all weekend to prove myself."*
- **Unconditional worth:** *"This report wasn't my best. The feedback stings, and it's useful. I'll fix it on Monday. My value isn't up for debate here."*

The second woman isn't less committed. She's just not under attack from the inside.

## Your worth timeline

Our beliefs about our worth are formed over many years, through family, school, faith communities, culture, early jobs and relationships. Understanding where yours came from helps you decide which beliefs to keep.

# [Module 2]{.num}The inner critic and the imposter

You've just been promoted. Your manager congratulates you, your colleagues send messages, your mother tells everyone at church. And there's a voice inside you saying: *They've made a mistake. Soon they'll find out you're not that good.*

That feeling has a name. The *imposter phenomenon* was first described in the late 1970s by psychologists studying high-achieving women who, despite clear evidence of success, believed they were frauds. Since then, research has found it across genders, professions and cultures. It's especially common among people who are the "first" or the "only" in their environment: the first in their family to go to university, the only woman on the leadership team, the youngest person in the room.

If you feel like an imposter, you're in very good company. And it doesn't mean you are one.

## How the imposter shows up

Imposter feelings tend to drive one of a few patterns. See which feel familiar:

- **The perfectionist.** If it's not flawless, it's a failure. You over-prepare and still feel unprepared.
- **The expert.** You feel you must know everything before you begin. You collect certificates but hesitate to apply.
- **The soloist.** Asking for help feels like proof you're not good enough, so you do everything yourself.
- **The superwoman.** You measure your worth by how many roles you handle perfectly at once: career, mother, daughter, friend, volunteer.
- **The natural genius.** If something doesn't come easily, you assume you're just not good at it.

## Talking back to the critic

You don't need to silence your inner critic completely. That rarely works. Instead, you can learn to respond to her with evidence and perspective.

When the critic speaks, try this four-step response:

1. **Name it.** *"That's my imposter talking."*
2. **Check the evidence.** *"What facts support this? What facts contradict it?"*
3. **Widen the lens.** *"Is it possible that I'm new to this and still learning, and that's normal?"*
4. **Choose your action.** *"What would I do right now if I believed I belonged here?"*

## The evidence log

Your brain is wired to remember criticism more vividly than praise. To balance that, you need to collect evidence deliberately. Keep a running log of:

- Positive feedback (save the emails and screenshots).
- Problems you solved.
- Things you learned quickly.
- Times people sought your advice.
- Results you contributed to.

When the imposter gets loud, read the log.

# [Module 3]{.num}Own your strengths

Ask a woman what she's good at and she'll often hesitate, then say something like, "Well, I'm quite organised, I suppose." Ask her what she needs to improve and she'll give you a detailed list in seconds.

Many of us were raised to be modest, and modesty can be a beautiful thing. But there's a difference between humility and invisibility. You can't make good career decisions, negotiate fairly or lead well if you don't clearly know what you bring.

## Why strengths are hard to see

Often, our greatest strengths are the things that come most easily to us. Because they feel effortless, we assume they're ordinary. *"Everyone can calm down an angry client." "Everyone can see the gaps in a plan."* They can't. That ease is a signal of a strength, not a sign of its unimportance.

## Three ways to find your strengths

**1. Look at your energy.** Which tasks make you lose track of time? Which leave you more energised than when you started?

**2. Look at your results.** Where have you consistently delivered? What do people thank you for?

**3. Ask other people.** This is the most powerful, and the most uncomfortable, exercise in this module. It's based on an approach researchers call the *Reflected Best Self* exercise, and many women describe it as transformative.

# [Module 4]{.num}Speaking up

Grace is a senior analyst at a large firm. In meetings, she often has the answer to the problem being discussed. But by the time she has phrased it perfectly in her head and waited for a polite gap, the conversation has moved on. Sometimes, ten minutes later, a male colleague says almost exactly what she had been thinking, and everyone nods.

If this sounds familiar, you're not imagining it. Many women report being interrupted more often, having their ideas attributed to others and being judged more harshly for being assertive. These are real dynamics. And while changing workplace culture is a bigger task, there are practical things you can do to make sure your voice is heard.

## Before the meeting

- **Prepare one point.** Decide on one contribution you'll make, and write it down in one sentence.
- **Speak early.** The longer you wait, the harder it gets. Aim to say something, even a question, in the first ten minutes.
- **Find an ally.** Agree with a colleague to amplify each other's points: *"I want to come back to what Grace said, because I think it's important."*

## In the meeting

- **Drop the softeners.** Words like *just*, *actually*, *sorry*, *I might be wrong but* and *does that make sense?* can weaken a strong point. You don't have to remove them all, but notice how often you use them.
- **Lead with your point, then explain.** *"I recommend we delay the launch by two weeks. Here's why..."* rather than a long build-up.
- **Handle interruptions calmly.** *"I'd like to finish my point,"* said warmly but firmly, followed by finishing your point.
- **Reclaim your idea.** *"Thanks for picking up on the idea I raised earlier. To build on it..."*

## When your voice shakes

Confidence doesn't mean the absence of nerves. Many experienced speakers still feel their heart race before they talk. A few slow breaths, both feet on the floor and a sip of water can help you feel more grounded. Then speak anyway. Your nervous system learns from experience that speaking up is survivable.

# [Module 5]{.num}Asking for what you're worth

Asking for money is where self-worth meets reality. Whether it's a salary negotiation, a request for a raise or setting your consulting rates, many women undervalue themselves, often without realising it.

There are complicated reasons for this, from social expectations that women should be grateful rather than demanding, to real-world experiences of being penalised for negotiating. You can't fix all of that alone. But you can prepare well enough that you walk in knowing your value and leave knowing you asked for it.

## Do your research

Never walk into a negotiation guessing. Find out:

- What do similar roles pay in your industry, city and level of experience? Check salary surveys, recruitment agency reports and job adverts, and ask peers you trust.
- What value have you added? Quantify it if you can: money saved, revenue earned, time reduced, people developed.
- What's your walk-away point? Decide this before the conversation, when you're calm.

## Build your brag document

A *brag document* is a running list of your achievements, kept throughout the year. It sounds uncomfortable, but it's simply good record-keeping. When review time comes, you're not trying to remember what you did in March.

Include: projects led, results achieved, problems solved, skills learned, positive feedback, extra responsibilities taken on and people you've mentored.

## Scripts that help

**Asking for a raise:**

> "Over the last year, I've taken on [responsibility] and delivered [result]. Based on my research, the market rate for this role is between [range]. I'd like to discuss bringing my salary to [figure]. What would it take to make that happen?"

**Responding to a job offer:**

> "Thank you, I'm really excited about this role. Based on my experience and the scope of the position, I was expecting something closer to [figure]. Is there flexibility there?"

**Setting your rates as a freelancer:**

> "My rate for this kind of project is [figure]. That includes [deliverables]."

Then stop talking. Silence is not your enemy. Let them respond.

## When the answer is no

Sometimes the answer will be no, or not yet. Ask: *"What would I need to demonstrate to reach that level? Can we agree a timeline to revisit it?"* Then write it down and follow up. A no today can become a yes in six months, if you keep the conversation open.

# [Module 6]{.num}Boundaries and the art of no

It's hard to feel worthy when your time, energy and kindness are constantly being taken for granted. Many confident-looking women are quietly exhausted, because they never learned to say no.

Boundaries aren't about being difficult. They're about being clear. A boundary tells other people how to treat you, and it tells *you* that your needs matter too.

## Signs you need stronger boundaries

- You often say yes and then resent it.
- You feel responsible for other people's feelings.
- You're the person everyone comes to, and nobody checks on you.
- You work evenings and weekends because "someone has to."
- You feel guilty when you rest.

## How to say no, kindly

A good no is short, warm and clear. You don't owe a long explanation. Over-explaining invites negotiation.

- *"Thank you for asking me. I can't take that on right now."*
- *"I'd love to help, but my plate is full this month. Could we revisit in March?"*
- *"That doesn't work for me, but here's what I can do..."*
- *"Let me check my priorities and get back to you tomorrow."* (This buys you time to decide calmly.)

## Boundaries at home and in family

Boundaries with family can be the hardest of all, especially in cultures where women are expected to carry a great deal. Requests for money, time, hosting and caregiving can quietly overwhelm you.

You can love your family deeply and still say: *"I can contribute this amount this month, and no more."* or *"I can host on Sunday, but I need everyone to bring a dish."* Clear and kind is not selfish. It's sustainable.

# [Module 7]{.num}Self-respect in everyday care

How you treat yourself every day sends a message to your own mind about your worth. When you skip meals, sleep four hours, ignore your health and speak to yourself harshly, you're reinforcing the belief that you come last.

Self-care isn't only bubble baths and spa days, though those are lovely. At its heart, it's self-respect in action. It looks like keeping the promises you make to yourself.

## The basics matter more than you think

Confidence is very hard to access when you're exhausted, hungry or unwell. Before you try to fix your mindset, check the foundations:

- **Sleep.** Are you getting enough rest most nights?
- **Food.** Are you eating regular, nourishing meals?
- **Movement.** Does your body get to move most days?
- **Health.** Are you keeping up with check-ups and taking care of any ongoing conditions?
- **Joy.** When did you last do something purely because you enjoy it?

## How you carry yourself

The way you hold your body can influence how you feel. Many women find that standing or sitting upright, breathing slowly, making eye contact and taking up a little more physical space helps them feel more grounded before a difficult conversation. This isn't about faking anything. It's about giving your body cues of safety and steadiness.

## Promises to yourself

Every time you keep a promise to yourself, even a small one, you build self-trust. And self-trust is the root of confidence. Start small: *"I'll drink water before coffee." "I'll stop working at 6 p.m. on Wednesdays." "I'll walk for 20 minutes three times this week."* Then keep the promise.

# [Module 8]{.num}Being visible

You can be excellent at your work and still be overlooked if nobody knows what you're doing. Visibility isn't vanity. It's how opportunities find you.

For many women, the idea of "self-promotion" feels uncomfortable, even distasteful. We hope our work will speak for itself. Sometimes it does. Often, it needs a little help.

## Reframe visibility as service

When you share what you know, you're not just promoting yourself. You're helping others. A LinkedIn post about how you navigated a difficult project could be exactly what another woman needs to read today. A talk about your career journey could show a younger woman what's possible.

Visibility is generosity with a byline.

## Simple ways to become more visible

- **At work:** share short updates on your progress with your manager. Volunteer to present your team's results. Ask for stretch projects.
- **On LinkedIn:** share one post a week about something you learned, a project you're proud of or a perspective from your field. Comment thoughtfully on others' posts.
- **In your community:** speak on a panel, mentor someone, write an article, join a professional association.

## A simple structure for a LinkedIn post

1. **A hook:** a single honest line that makes someone want to read more.
2. **A short story or observation** from your work or life.
3. **The lesson** you took from it.
4. **A question** inviting others to share their experience.

You don't need to post every day. You don't need to go viral. You just need to show up consistently enough that people know who you are and what you care about.
